## Surgery 1: Carotid endarterectomy
Many seniors are told they have narrowing in the arteries of the neck, the carotid arteries, and that this raises their risk of stroke. And that surgery can fix it.
The operation is called a carotid endarterectomy. The surgeon opens the artery in your neck, removes the plaque, and closes it again. In the right patient, it reduces stroke risk.
But the benefit depends almost entirely on one thing: whether you've already had symptoms.
If you've had a transient ischemic attack, a "mini-stroke", with sudden arm weakness, slurred speech or temporary vision loss, and your carotid is more than 70% narrowed, the evidence strongly supports surgery. The NASCET trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1991, showed a large reduction in stroke for these patients.
But what if you've never had symptoms, and a routine ultrasound just found narrowing?
✎ For people without symptoms, the ACST-1 trial, published in The Lancet in 2004, found that surgery reduced the five-year risk of stroke from about 12% to about 6%: a real benefit, of about 5 percentage points, already counting the risk of the operation itself.
✎ But that operation carried about a 3% risk of stroke or death around the time of surgery, in expert centers. Hospitals that do fewer of these operations tend to have worse results. That's why guidelines say surgery for patients without symptoms only makes sense when the surgical risk is very low, below about 3%, and the patient has a good life expectancy.
✎ And medical treatment has improved a lot since those trials: statins, blood pressure control and antiplatelet drugs like aspirin. For many people without symptoms, especially those with a higher surgical risk, this medical route is a reasonable option, and new trials are comparing both strategies right now.
I'm not telling you to refuse this surgery. I'm telling you to write down two questions: Am I symptomatic or asymptomatic? And what is the complication rate for this procedure, at this hospital?
A confident, experienced surgeon will answer both directly.

## Surgery 2: Spinal cord stimulator
Chronic back pain and nerve pain in the legs affect sleep, mood, independence… everything.
When injections stop working, a new option may appear: a spinal cord stimulator. Electrodes near the spinal cord, and a small battery-powered generator under the skin, sending pulses that interrupt pain signals.
For some patients, it genuinely helps.
✎ But it's an implanted device, and devices can fail. Published reviews estimate that roughly 30 to 40% of patients have some complication over time: wires that move or break, device problems or battery replacement. Newer systems seem to do better, but long-term data are still limited.
That means some people will need at least one more procedure. And infection, while not common, can be serious, especially with diabetes or poor circulation. If an infected device must be removed, that's another surgery.
✎ Results also vary a lot from person to person, and today we can't reliably predict who will benefit most.
Often, the stimulator is offered because a previous back surgery didn't work. If that's your case, ask: What does the evidence show, not just in the first months, but at two and five years, for someone with my diagnosis and my age?
And ask about the alternatives: structured pain rehabilitation programs, radiofrequency ablation for specific pain sources, and physical therapy combined with pain education.

## Surgery 3: Radical prostatectomy
This section is for every man over 60, and for everyone who loves one.
When a doctor says "prostate cancer", the instinct is immediate: take it out.
I understand. But here's what large trials found about localized prostate cancer, especially low-risk cancer.
✎ The PIVOT trial, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2012, followed 731 men for more than a decade. Half had surgery to remove the prostate; half were observed. Surgery did not significantly reduce overall deaths or prostate cancer deaths. Updated results in 2017, with nearly 20 years of data, said the same. Any possible benefit appeared in men with intermediate-risk cancer, not low-risk cancer.
✎ Then the ProtecT trial, in 2016, followed more than 1,600 men across three groups: active monitoring, surgery and radiation. At 10 years, about 1% died of prostate cancer in each group. The monitoring group did have more cancer progression, so monitoring must be done carefully.
And surgery has consequences monitoring doesn't. In ProtecT, at six months, about 46% of men who had surgery used pads every day for urine leakage, compared with about 4% in the monitoring group. It improves over time, but not for everyone. And erectile problems are even more common.
✎ For a 72-year-old man, these aren't footnotes. They change daily life.
Active surveillance is not giving up. It's a structured plan: regular PSA tests, MRI, biopsies when needed, and clear criteria for when to treat.
If you or someone you love gets this diagnosis, ask: Based on my Gleason score, my PSA and my overall health, am I a candidate for active surveillance? What would that monitoring look like?
✎ Low-risk prostate cancer usually grows slowly. A calm, informed decision is usually better than a rushed one.

## Surgery 4: Both knees or hips at once
Let me be clear: hip and knee replacements, done thoughtfully, are among the most successful surgeries in modern medicine.
What deserves a careful look is a specific version: replacing both knees, or both hips, in one operation, under one anesthesia.
It sounds logical: one anesthesia, one recovery. But for a 74-year-old with high blood pressure, diabetes or heart disease, the math can change.
Doing both at once means more blood loss, and more strain on the heart and kidneys at the same moment. The risk of blood clots in the legs and lungs is also higher.
✎ A large study by Memtsoudis and colleagues, analyzing millions of hospital discharges, found more in-hospital complications and higher mortality with both knees at once than with one knee, especially in older patients and those with other conditions.
✎ In younger, healthier patients, the difference is much smaller. The concern is mainly for older people with other health problems.
And there's rehabilitation: with both legs operated at once, both hurt at the same time. If you live alone, falls and readmissions become more likely.
So ask: What does the data show for both at once versus one at a time, for someone my age with my conditions? What is my real rehabilitation plan, where I live, with who's available to help? And what about doing them some months apart?

## Surgery 5: Aortic aneurysm, EVAR or open repair
An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a bulge in the body's main artery. If a large one ruptures, it's often fatal. Repairing it at the right time saves lives.
There are two ways to repair it. Open surgery, through the abdomen, with weeks of recovery. Or EVAR: a stent graft placed through small incisions in the groin, often home in a few days.
The EVAR-1 trial, published in The Lancet in 2004, showed much lower early mortality with EVAR: 1.7% versus 4.7%.
✎ But its long-term follow-up, published in 2016, found that after about eight years, survival was better with open repair, mainly because of late problems with the stent and late ruptures. And EVAR needed more repeat procedures.
✎ So age matters. For an older patient with other conditions, EVAR's early safety is often the deciding factor. For a healthier person in their 60s, with many years ahead, open repair may last better, if they can tolerate the operation.
And EVAR is not "fix it and forget it". It requires lifelong imaging checkups.
✎ And not every aneurysm needs surgery right away. Guidelines generally recommend repair from about 5.5 cm in men, and about 5 cm in women. Below that, regular ultrasound checkups and good blood pressure control are usually the right strategy.
If you're told an aneurysm below that size needs urgent surgery, ask what specific reason puts you in a different category.

## The 4 questions to ask before any major surgery
Knowledge that stays in your head doesn't protect you. Knowledge you bring to the consultation, written down, does.
Question one, the one I promised: What happens if I don't have this surgery in the next six months?
✎ A good surgeon will give you a clear answer. If nothing becomes urgent, you have time: for a second opinion, for alternatives, to prepare your body. If it is urgent, you'll understand why.
Question two: Is there a less invasive or non-surgical alternative, and what does the evidence show for someone my age?
Question three: What will recovery realistically look like for me, with my health and my living situation? If you live alone, what help will you need, and for how long?
Question four: What are your numbers? What are your results with this procedure in patients over 70?
✎ Experienced surgeons know their results. If the answer is vague, a second opinion is reasonable.
You're not being difficult when you ask these questions. You're being the kind of informed patient good medicine depends on.
